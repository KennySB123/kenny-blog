For all of human history, we have been the only necessary recipients of our attempts to define understanding. There has been informal and formal research on animals too, which has satisfied curiosity and showed us that man’s best friends know more than we originally thought. However, even then we still didn’t need to know how animals understood us, only if they did, and could be trained to understand more to become even more useful.

Most people probably know where this argument is going and expect the dreaded two letters to be uttered. Lampshading aside, AI is indeed here, and since the groundbreaking mathematics advancements such as the navier stokes equation which went unsolved for 100 years, this question is once again in the spotlight.

“Yes it worked it out, but it was by brute force, it only combined together existing ideas, it doesn’t really understand maths.”

I think this is representative of several interrelated arguments for why despite the ever increasing capabilities and volume of discoveries, we should expect human supremacy to remain. The issue is, for this argument to hold water at least two things have to be true simultaneously:

1. AI does not understand things in the true sense of the word
2. It matters that AI does not understand  things

Given what has already been achieved with results like alphafold, 2 already seems to be creaking under the growing pile of evidence.

I think 1 is far more interesting though and more directly addresses the core objection to AI as an entity that possesses understanding.

Firstly, how do we define understanding? On first attempt I would say it is 

“A working model of a subject matter that allows someone to make predictions and calculations about the subject and related elements.”

 This of course completely misses what matters to people which is the *feeling* of understanding, the “aha” moment where everything clicks after repeated failed efforts and straining your mind until it all falls into place. This is so visceral, so satisfying, and universally appreciated as to become an equivalent with what we mean when we say understanding. We have a particular association between the experience of understanding and the benefits and practical possibilities it brings. 

The issue of course is, AI models cannot feel anything, so what now? Does the conversation end because our experience of understanding is not equivalent to machines? Clearly we need to understand what *is* possible for AI. So let’s go back to our initial definition. Immediately, a word looks ripe for replacement “someone” should become “something” since there’s no clear reason why understanding would be solely restricted to biological organisms. Making predictions and calculations is certainly something AI can do already, so on an outcome based definition, it seems to pass the test.

Perhaps the definition of understanding is wrong though or I am strawmanning the arguments, let’s take each in turn.

Dictionary definitions are interesting but they do not hold anything substantially different from my own definition. Merriam-Webster points to “ a mental grasp” as the meaning and Cambridge as “knowledge about a subject, situation etc. or about how something works. 

Pointing to a “mental grasp” is a neat metaphor which I think accurately describes the feeling of gaining an understanding of something. However, it is completely human centric, not previously an issue, but clearly incompatible with a serious attempt of defining it more universally.

Cambridge instead argues it’s more about knowledge, particularly relating to how something works. I think the specificity about how things work is the crux of the definition, since understanding must be distinct from knowledge. 

From these definitions the next step seems to be to test if AI can know how something works. Now this is where all the trouble lies. If we were to apply the same question to a person and ask them to prove they understand something we would run into the immediate problem of externally verifying the mind. Anyone else’s mind is for us still a black box, we can track brain waves and electricity, but this is not the same thing as being inside someone’s mind. So we do what school does, test people. By asking questions, and perhaps ensuring they explain their answers, we can mutually agree if someone seems to have a grasp of a subject. 

If someone asked us to perform this same test, the same principles would apply, except we would also be able to add our subjective experience of understanding, the “aha” moment, for our internal verification. Now finally, let’s ask AI to answer questions and explain their answers. At least for areas like maths this is not going to be a problem basically regardless of difficulty. However, when the correct answers are returned including reasonable explanation, we will answer, “it’s just predicting characters, it can’t actually understand what it’s saying.” As opposed to humans where we know how we understand things?

I think here lies the central issue, beyond our cognition of our own understanding, we do not have a complete scientific model for how understanding works in the brain. What we do think is eerily similar to how, AI models appear to work. Our brain is constantly making predictions about the outside world based on external stimuli and internal thoughts. This is happening across billions of interconnected neurons and ultimately means we can think and understand things. Meanwhile AI has weights of billions of parameters and is constantly predicting the next token based on the previous one and the context available to it internally including what was taken as input like files. 

Clearly we have AI beat when it comes to the experience of understanding things. However, when we try to go deeper into the science of understanding, I think this quality becomes increasingly hard to withhold from AI. Certainly AI still makes mistakes, even basic ones, as humans often do. However, the recent improvements in capability and reliability do not seem to be the arrival of understanding but the ever increasingly obvious proof that the understanding has been there all along just in smaller quantities in less domains. 

Just like AI has confronted our understanding of understanding. I am sure it will hold up a mirror to more qualities like empathy and compassion, and maybe we will find that what we thought to be uniquely humans, may be something far more accessible.
