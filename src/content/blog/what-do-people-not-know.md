No one else knows exactly what I know. That has to be true, since there will always be mundane details about my life which most people would have no reason to know or care about. Where I was at 9PM yesterday is of course not what people mean when they talk about someone's knowledge (unless you're the police, and I'm in a terrible set of circumstances). Rather, 'What I know about life' is a bit closer to the heart of what matters. There are still a huge variety of facts, beliefs, and understandings that exist between life philosophy and breakfast. Altogether, this makes up the footprint of my total experience which could not be replicated without experiencing life exactly as I have.

This of course applies to every other person alive or dead. There will always be areas of overlap, presumably more as you get closer both in culture, education, and the era you grew up in. I could say e=mc² and if someone else agreed, we would reasonably have to put this in the overlap of knowledge. In the simplest case, we can see there is a venn diagram of knowledge uniquely held by me, another person, and then shared by both of us. This will then extend out with overlapping lines for every other person alive, creating an unbelievably complicated network of knowledge.

Why does it matter if someone knows something though? Ignoring ideas of "knowledge for knowledge's sake", most people can make better decisions with better information, or do things otherwise impossible. How many people through history have died of a heart attack because no one knew how to do CPR? It's pretty clear that knowing things is pretty important, but not everyone knows everything, and like my breakfast, not everything is worth knowing equally.

There is a huge amount of effort spent trying to colour the venn diagram of as many people as possible with the knowledge think is most important to have. After all, why would we subject every child in the western world to school, if we didn't think learning the fundamentals of language, mathematics, science, history, and more was important. Then yet more lessons have been imparted through stories and warnings about good behaviour and morals, do not kill, do not steal, treat others as you would wish they would treat you.

Altogether this creates a shared cultural baseline where any person can reasonably expect someone in similar circumstances as them to share an understanding of the most important aspects of society. This is probably why 'culture shock' is so common and can be so disorientating. An example I heard was **Janteloven** which describes Norways cultural norm of discouraging boasting and individualism, succinctly, do not think **you** are more important than **we** are. Compare this to America where the 'American dream' is universally known, and encourages making a success of oneself regardless of where you started in life.

These attitudes are mostly contradictory, or you could certainly find two people holding diametrically opposed variations of these beliefs. Then, the question becomes not 'What does the other person not know?' but 'What belief does the other person hold instead of the other possible contradictory ones.' Polarisation is widely observed to be increasing across the western world, and social media is being blamed, an explanation most people would be immediately inclined to believe. Two people can have personal algorithms that have been trained to learn to provide them content supporting the opposite positions on any given range of societal issues. With enough time, these beliefs will be reinforced, and without any good faith medium to discuss these views, they harden until dialogue appears impossible. Effectively creating a bipolar culture where people's venn diagrams are eroded until there are two groups and each continually tries to pull fence sitters into their orbit.

Society was of course not perfect before, racism was embedded in America's culture in many places, and the lack of algorithms only meant there were more people in the same group, as opposite views struggled against the monoculture.

Bridging this gap seems to be one of the more important questions of our time, but the solution cannot just be averaging the extremes of opinion. This is where most of the danger seems to lie, both sides can simply say. "I am right, they should share my views" and inevitably nothing and no one changes. Maybe beliefs becoming core to tribal identity is the issue, since they, unlike facts (or the closest we have to them epistemically), can be contradictory. It's not surprising that allowing unsubstantiated beliefs to become social currency has done so much harm to social cohesion. When people are arguing about any given issue, especially online, the goal is usually closer to 'winning' than 'reaching a shared understanding' or even worse, *changing your mind*. 

I definitely enjoyed trying to approach this from first principles, but even  the most clear understanding of the problem, is insufficient without action. At least now I think some prerequisites needed to bridge people's beliefs and knowledge are more clear.

1. Making it safe to change your view - detribalising beliefs, or creating a new tribe for people making this change
2. Creating forums of debate that include facts, information, and research that are not curated for a particular view point
3. Facts, information, and research presented are as objectively correct as possible, and can be verified by both sides
